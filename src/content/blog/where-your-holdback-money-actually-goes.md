Every contractor eventually asks the same question: the job made money, so where did it go? Nine times out of ten, the answer is sitting in a holdback account, a stack of progress nobody has billed yet, or job costing nobody has looked at since the estimate. Here is the plain version of how those three things fit together.

## What a holdback actually is

A holdback is money your client is entitled to keep back from every progress payment until the job is finished and a waiting period has passed. It is not a penalty and it is not optional. It is built into standard construction contracts and, in most provinces, into the lien legislation itself.

How much gets held back, and for how long, is set by your contract and your provincial lien rules, not by habit. <span class="ph">Confirm your holdback percentage and lien period</span> before you assume this job matches the last one.

### Why it feels like the money disappears

Most owners do not lose sleep over the holdback line itself. They lose sleep over the gap between what a job is worth and what is sitting in the bank, which is usually a few things stacked on top of each other:

- The holdback itself, sitting with the client until the waiting period clears, even though your crew and suppliers are already paid.
- Holdbacks you owe your own subcontractors, which do not disappear just because your client is holding yours.
- Progress that has happened but has not been billed yet, because the draw only went out for what was signed off two weeks ago.
- Change orders still being priced, sitting in limbo instead of on an invoice.

## How progress billing actually works

Progress billing is the mechanism for turning finished work into cash before the job wraps. It runs the same rough sequence on almost every project, whether anyone writes it down or not:

1. Measure the work. Before you can bill, someone agrees how much of the job is done, by percentage complete, by unit, or against a schedule of values.
2. Submit the draw. The progress claim goes to the client or their consultant, itemized against the contract, not as a lump guess.
3. Get it certified. The client, or their payment certifier, reviews the draw and sometimes trims it before sign-off.
4. Invoice the certified amount, holdback deducted, and start the clock on payment terms.
5. Collect, then repeat next month, while the holdback keeps building until substantial completion.

### Where job costing comes in

Progress billing tells you what you can invoice. Job costing tells you what you actually made. The two numbers are rarely the same, and the gap is where a job quietly loses money without anyone noticing until it is finished.

Job costing means tracking labour, material and equipment against each job, not the business as a whole, so a slow job cannot hide behind a fast one. The table below is a simplified example of what that tracking looks like once a few draws have gone out.
